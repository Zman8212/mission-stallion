Weighted Sleep Debt (h/night) — Last 14 Days
What this is. A flat 7-day average has a cliff: Jul 27's 9.5h night counts at full weight today and then vanishes completely tomorrow, so the number moves on its own without you doing anything. This metric fixes that. Every night counts a little less the further back it is, fading smoothly instead of dropping off a ledge: last night 100%, three nights ago 75%, a week ago 37%, two weeks ago 14%. A night's weight roughly halves every five days.
Why 14 days and not 7 or 30. Van Dongen 2003 restricted people for 14 straight nights and the damage was still accumulating linearly at day 14: no plateau, no adaptation. Worse, subjective sleepiness saturated early while performance kept falling, which means chronic debt is something you cannot feel and have to compute. A flat 7-day window hard-zeroes 37% of the history that still matters. A 30-day window adds about 2% and has no experimental support at all, because nobody has ever run the study past 14 days. The 7-day fade constant is not invented: it is the decay fitted to the lab data in the Unified Model of Performance, validated across five studies from four labs.
One night just did more damage than the whole of last week: the debt jumped +1.17 to +1.51, the largest single-day move the metric has made and the highest value it has ever printed. The seven measured nights read 9.5, 6.6, 7.7, 6.4, 5.8, 7.0 and 4.3h. That last one is the story. A 3:14am onset to a 7:52am wake is 4h38m in bed, so even at 92% efficiency there was no night to have: this is not a sleep-quality problem, it is a nothing-was-attempted problem. It cost 3.7h against target in one go, and because the weighted metric counts last night at full weight, the whole of Monday's 9.5h credit was wiped and then some. Read the week as two halves. Monday to Thursday: four straight pre-midnight onsets, the best front-end stretch the page has recorded. Friday to Sunday: 1:37am, 12:56am, 3:14am, each later than the last. Both flat averages fell again, 14-day to 6.62h and 7-day to 6.77h, now 74 to 83 minutes short. Variance went the wrong way too, ±1.62h from ±1.33, the worst reading of the program. Windred 2024 (UK Biobank) found sleep regularity predicted mortality more strongly than sleep duration, and a week that runs 9.5h to 4.3h is the definition of irregular. The encouraging half is genuinely encouraging and should not be lost: the weekday routine works. The problem now has a shape, and the shape is Friday, Saturday and Sunday night.

The sleep target moved on Jul 13, and it moved up: 8.0h of actual sleep, 9h in bed. The old 7.5h target was too low and it was quietly flattering the page. The lab studies that measured real sleep with electrodes, not self-report, converge on ~8h: Van Dongen 2003 modelled sleep need at 8.16h, Belenky's 9h-in-bed group achieved 7.93h, and Rupp 2009 gave people 10 hours in bed at home and they still only slept 7.99h. That last one is the ceiling: you cannot brute-force past about 8h of actual sleep, and trying tanks your efficiency. Note also what the athlete literature actually says. The famous Stanford "athletes need 10 hours" study (Mah 2011) used 10h as the time-in-bed goal; the players' measured sleep went 6.7h to 8.5h. Nobody in that study slept 10 hours. At Zak's 90% efficiency, 8.0h asleep needs 8h53m in bed, so a 9:00am wake means lights out by midnight. That is the single number this whole page now hangs on.
