Weighted Sleep Debt (h/night) — Last 14 Days
What this is. A flat 7-day average has a cliff: Jul 22's 5.6h night counts at full weight today and then vanishes completely tomorrow, so the number improves on its own without you doing anything. This metric fixes that. Every night counts a little less the further back it is, fading smoothly instead of dropping off a ledge: last night 100%, three nights ago 75%, a week ago 37%, two weeks ago 14%. A night's weight roughly halves every five days.
Why 14 days and not 7 or 30. Van Dongen 2003 restricted people for 14 straight nights and the damage was still accumulating linearly at day 14: no plateau, no adaptation. Worse, subjective sleepiness saturated early while performance kept falling, which means chronic debt is something you cannot feel and have to compute. A flat 7-day window hard-zeroes 37% of the history that still matters. A 30-day window adds about 2% and has no experimental support at all, because nobody has ever run the study past 14 days. The 7-day fade constant is not invented: it is the decay fitted to the lab data in the Unified Model of Performance, validated across five studies from four labs.
One good night did not fix it, and today's number is the proof: the debt ticked back up from +1.03h to +1.07h. The seven measured nights read 5.6, 7.8, 5.1, 7.6, 5.6, 9.5 and 6.6h. Monday's 9.5h was worth a lot because recency is weighted, but a 6.6h night behind it still runs 1.4h under target, and the metric immediately gave part of the credit back. That is the whole point of weighting: a catch-up night buys you one day of relief, not a reset. The flat averages barely moved either, 14-day 6.92h and 7-day 6.85h, still 65 to 70 minutes a night short, and variance stayed pinned at ±1.58h, the worst of the program. Windred 2024 (UK Biobank) found sleep regularity predicted mortality more strongly than sleep duration, so the swing is not a cosmetic complaint. What is genuinely new this week sits in the onset column rather than the duration one: 9:48pm then 11:26pm, the two earliest starts of the seven, and the first back-to-back pre-midnight pair the page has recorded. Last night still came up short only because the wake landed at 6:31am. Hold that onset, move the wake back to 9:00, and the arithmetic produces an 8h night without anything else changing.

The lab studies that measured real sleep with electrodes, not self-report, converge on ~8h: Van Dongen 2003 modelled sleep need at 8.16h, Belenky's 9h-in-bed group achieved 7.93h, and Rupp 2009 gave people 10 hours in bed at home and they still only slept 7.99h. That last one is the ceiling: you cannot brute-force past about 8h of actual sleep, and trying tanks your efficiency. Note also what the athlete literature actually says. The famous Stanford "athletes need 10 hours" study (Mah 2011) used 10h as the time-in-bed goal; the players' measured sleep went 6.7h to 8.5h. Nobody in that study slept 10 hours. At Zak's 90% efficiency, 8.0h asleep needs 8h53m in bed, so a 9:00am wake means lights out by midnight. That is the single number this whole page now hangs on.
